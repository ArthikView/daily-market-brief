Gold: why it rose, why it fell, and what the data says next

Gold's extraordinary rally and sharp correction are best understood through a connected set of forces: real interest rates, the US dollar, inflation expectations, central-bank purchases and investor flows.

BY CHANDAN PATHAK [ ECONOMIST ] · DATA AS OF 21 NOVEMBER 2026
← BACK TO COVERAGE

Gold spent 2025 and early 2026 on a historic run, then gave back a substantial part of that advance. The key lesson is that gold is not simply an inflation hedge or a safe-haven asset. Its short-run price is also highly sensitive to the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset, the dollar and changes in investor positioning.

01 / Twenty-six years of gold in perspective

The long-run history shows that gold can move through very different regimes. The forces behind each period are more informative than any single price level.

PeriodApproximate price movementDominant drivers cited
1971–1980$35 → ~$850/ozEnd of the dollar–gold link, oil shocks and double-digit inflation.
1980–2001→ ~$250–300/ozVery high US policy rates, a stronger dollar, falling inflation and central-bank selling.
2001–2011~$270 → ~$1,900/ozWeaker dollar, the global financial crisis, near-zero rates, quantitative easing and the growth of gold ETFs.
2011–2015About −45%Fed tapering, stronger dollar, ETF outflows and easing crisis fears.
2019–2020→ ~$2,075/ozCOVID shock and very low real yields.
2022–Jan 2026~$1,800 → $5,589/ozRecord central-bank purchases, geopolitical risk, rate-cut expectations and the so-called debasement trade.
Feb–Nov 2026$5,589 → ~$3,770/ozStronger dollar, a more hawkish Fed outlook, oil-led inflation concerns, ETF outflows and profit-taking.

The recurring relationship is conditional, not mechanical: gold often benefits when real yields and the dollar fall, and can come under pressure when they rise. The 1980 and 2013 episodes are useful historical comparisons for the role of rates and the dollar. They do not prove that every future correction will follow the same path.

02 / What drove the 2025–26 boom and correction?

2025 · THE RALLY

Gold began the year near $2,624/oz and, according to the supplied research, recorded repeated highs through the year, including its first close above $4,000 in the second half.

JAN 2026 · THE PEAK

Gold crossed $5,000 and reached a reported peak of $5,589 on 28 January. Profit-taking followed; on 30 January gold reportedly fell about 10% in a day, while silver fell about 28%.

FEB 2026 · OIL SHOCK

After the Iran conflict began on 28 February, oil reportedly moved above $100. Gold fell rather than rising, as inflation concerns and expectations of tighter monetary policy supported the dollar and weighed on bullion.

MAY–JUN 2026

US CPI was reported at 4.2% in May, the highest since April 2023. Gold slipped below $4,000 on 24 June; Asian gold ETFs reportedly recorded their first monthly outflow since August 2025.

NOV 2026 · SNAPSHOT

The supplied spot reference was about $3,770/oz on 18 November, roughly 32% below the January high.

The counter-intuitive episode is the oil shock. Geopolitical stress can create safe-haven demand, but an energy-price spike can also raise inflation expectations, push bond yields and the dollar higher, and reduce expectations of rate cuts. In the episode described here, those rate-and-currency channels appear to have outweighed the safe-haven impulse.

Economic mechanism: Gold pays no coupon. When inflation-adjusted yields on competing assets rise, the opportunity cost of holding gold can increase. A stronger US dollar can also make dollar-priced bullion more expensive for buyers using other currencies. These are important channels, not standalone laws that predict every daily move.

The research notes also describe a broad 2027 forecast range of roughly $4,100–$8,000 across institutions. Such a wide range is a reminder of how sensitive gold forecasts are to assumptions about real rates, central-bank demand, ETF flows and geopolitics. The notes attribute part of the remaining bullish case to central-bank purchases, with a cited expected pace of about 60 tonnes per month versus a recent pace around 85 tonnes.

Targets can change quickly. A forecast that was reasonable under one rate path can become stale after a change in inflation, oil prices or central-bank guidance. Always attach a date and institution to a price target.

These are conditional scenarios drawn from the supplied research, not probability-weighted forecasts or trading signals.

Indicators worth watching:

  • US real yields and Federal Reserve communication.
  • The US dollar index and broader currency conditions.
  • Monthly central-bank purchases reported by the World Gold Council.
  • Gold ETF holdings and investment flows.
  • Crude oil prices and inflation data.
  • For Indian prices, the rupee: a weaker rupee can cushion or amplify local gold prices even when international dollar gold is falling.
BOTTOM LINE

Central banks may support the long-run demand story. Rates and the dollar can dominate the short run.

The structural story is central banks becoming steady buyers, potentially providing a source of underlying demand. The cyclical story is interest rates and the US dollar, which the supplied data describes as headwinds during the recent correction. Neither force is guaranteed to prevail. Professional forecasts have already shifted materially, so a single price target deserves caution, not certainty.
